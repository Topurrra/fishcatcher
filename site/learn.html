Learn to spot a scam

The same tricks show up again and again. Once you know them, they are easy to catch.

Plain language, no jargon. A few minutes each.

Fake and lookalike links

Most scams start with a link that looks almost right. The trick is in the web address.

Read the address from right to left. The real owner is the part just before the first single slash, like paypal.com in paypal.com/login. Everything before it is a subdomain and can say anything.

  • login.paypal.com.secure-alert.ru is not PayPal. The real owner is secure-alert.ru.
  • paypa1.com uses a number 1 in place of the letter l.
  • paypal-support.com is a different site from paypal.com.

If a link's visible text says one site but it points somewhere else, that mismatch is a red flag. FishCatcher warns you about exactly this.

Fake login pages

A scam page can copy a real login screen perfectly. The page looks fine; the address is what gives it away.

  • Never log in from a link in an email or text. Open the site yourself, from a bookmark or by typing it.
  • If a page asks for your password and the address is not the real site, stop.
  • Real companies do not ask you to "verify" your password because of an urgent problem.

QR code scams

A QR code is just a link you cannot read with your eyes. Scammers put fake QR codes on posters, parking meters, and emails.

  • Before you act on a QR code, check the link it contains.
  • Be extra careful with codes that lead to a payment or a login.
  • FishCatcher can read a QR code for you and check the link, all on your device.

Device-code scams

Some sign-ins show you a short code to type on a real page, like linking a TV to your account. Scammers abuse this.

They send you a code and rush you to enter it at a real login page. If you do, you are approving their sign-in to your account.

Only enter a device code if you started the process yourself, from an app you opened. A code someone sent you is a trap.

MFA and proxy attacks

Even with two-factor authentication, a scam page can sit in the middle, pass your login to the real site, and steal the session. The defense is the same: check the address before you enter anything.

  • Passkeys and security keys are the strongest protection, because they refuse to work on the wrong site.
  • Be suspicious of any "urgent security upgrade" that sends you to a login page.

Dangerous downloads

A file named invoice.pdf can really be a program in disguise. Opening it runs the program.

  • Watch for double endings like photo.jpg.exe.
  • Do not open a "document" that turns out to be a program, unless you fully trust the source.
  • FishCatcher's download guard checks a download's real type and warns you if it does not match its name.

Golden rules

  • Slow down. Urgency is the scammer's main tool.
  • Check the web address before you type anything.
  • Open sites yourself instead of clicking links in messages.
  • No real company asks for your password or codes to "fix" an urgent problem.
  • When in doubt, close the tab. You lose nothing by leaving.
